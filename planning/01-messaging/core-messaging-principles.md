# Core Messaging Principles

**Status:** Approved v1.0  
**Purpose:** Practical copywriting rules for Hacks-a-Thon messaging, homepage copy, sales pages, content, and demos.

## 1. Lead with adoption, not the event format

Hacks-a-Thon is not primarily a hackathon product. It is a structured path to AI adoption.

The event format is the vehicle. The outcome is a team that feels more confident creating with AI.

Use language like:

- AI adoption
- employee confidence
- hands-on creation
- culture change
- organizational momentum

Use “hackathon” language sparingly, especially in hero copy.

## 2. Treat AI adoption as a people problem

The central problem is not that organizations lack access to AI tools. The problem is that most employees do not know where to begin, which tools to trust, or how AI applies to their actual work.

Hacks-a-Thon should frame the problem as:

- too many tools
- too much noise
- unclear starting points
- fear of looking behind
- uncertainty about what is actually useful

## 3. Confidence is the transformation

People do leave with information, examples, resources, and working projects.

But the deeper outcome is confidence.

The strongest messaging should make it clear that participants move from:

- unsure to capable
- passive observers to active creators
- tool-curious to project-ready
- overwhelmed to energized

## 4. Creation beats training

Do not position Hacks-a-Thon as another AI training course.

Training can explain what AI is. Creation proves what someone can do with it.

The brand should consistently reinforce that people learn faster when they build something real, personal, and useful.

## 5. Keep the human tone

The product should feel clear, encouraging, practical, and a little playful.

Avoid fear-based AI language, corporate transformation sludge, and overly technical explanations.

Hacks-a-Thon should sound like a smart creative leader helping a team get unstuck, not a consultant selling a framework.

## 6. Use “tools” more than “software”

“Software” can feel formal and product-heavy.

“Tools” feels more approachable, flexible, and creator-friendly.

Use “platform” when describing Hacks-a-Thon itself. Use “tools” when describing what participants create, use, or discover.

## 7. Be accurate about what participants create

Do not overstate that every participant creates an AI-powered tool.

More accurate language:

- projects
- tools
- websites
- workflows
- automations
- experiments
- prototypes
- solutions

Participants use AI to create. The thing they create may or may not itself be AI-powered.

## 8. Sell the shared experience

The product is not just individual skill-building. It is a shared organizational moment.

Messaging should reinforce that Hacks-a-Thon helps teams:

- learn together
- see what others are building
- normalize experimentation
- create internal proof
- build momentum across departments

## 9. Make self-service feel easy

The homepage should guide visitors toward exploration, not a heavy sales process.

Preferred primary CTA:

**See How It Works**

Secondary CTA direction:

- Explore the Admin Experience
- Experience the Participant Journey
- View the Playbook

## 10. Protect the Seven2 origin story

The Seven2 story is the proof source and emotional foundation.

The homepage should reference it enough to establish credibility, but the full story belongs on the About / Case Study page.

Homepage role: show that this came from a real team doing the work.

Case study role: tell the full origin story, participant transformation, and why it became a product.
